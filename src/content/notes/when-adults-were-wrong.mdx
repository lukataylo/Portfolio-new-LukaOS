I grew up on a steady diet of confident adult advice. Some of it was wisdom. A surprising amount was fear wearing wisdom's clothes — and the only way to tell the difference was to run the experiments myself.

Years later, I have results. A pattern emerged that I think generalises: the most confident warnings said more about the person giving them than about the world.

### "Big cities are cold. You won't make friends."

The city was always framed as anonymous — a place where connection happens only by luck. My measured experience is the opposite.

What's actually true: nobody shows up at your door with friendship. Connection in a city is pull, not push. Book clubs, events, saying yes, following up — invest the effort and people are remarkably open. The default is neutral, not hostile.

The city never failed me. Passive expectations would have.

### "It's hard to make it."

Technically true, the way all unfalsifiable statements are true. Life is expensive; trade-offs are real. But notice how the sentence gets used — as a full stop, not a description. It ends conversations instead of informing decisions.

I bought a home in the city centre and walk to work every day. No shortcut, no windfall — just explicit trade-offs made on purpose. I gave up square footage for proximity. Ego for time. Abstract status for daily quality of life.

"Hard" was never the operative variable. *Priced* was. Most things people call impossible are just priced in a currency they'd rather not pay.

### "You'll miss home. You'll regret not learning the grammar."

Delivered with total certainty. Falsified by simply living.

This is the category I find most interesting, because it isn't prediction at all — it's identity pressure disguised as prediction. Roots, loyalty, what a "good person" values: a moral story projected onto your choices, with "you'll regret it" as the enforcement mechanism.

### "You'll miss being a kid."

Presented as universal truth. I don't buy it, and I think I understand why it gets said.

Adults who say this are usually describing their own unresolved trade-offs — responsibility overload, anxiety, fatigue. That pain is real. But it's a statement about their adulthood, not about adulthood. Mine is better than childhood by every measure I care about: more agency, more choice, more self-respect. The complexity is the price, and it's worth it.

### The underlying mechanism

Run the post-mortem on enough bad advice and the same root causes keep appearing: projection, defensive pessimism, identity preservation, unprocessed regret.

Hence the title. Advice is autobiography. People narrate their own lives at you in the imperative mood.

### What I actually do now

I still listen — older people have real data. But I changed how I parse the input. For every piece of advice: is this observation or projection? Caution or fear? My path or their story?

The operational shift: treat inherited narratives as hypotheses, not laws. Some survived testing. Many didn't. There was never a way to know in advance — only the experiment.

Run yours.
