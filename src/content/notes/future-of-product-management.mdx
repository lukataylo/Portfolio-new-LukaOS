Here's a frame that's been clarifying for me: most product job titles aren't descriptions of skills. They're descriptions of *hand-offs*.

We split product work into PM, designer, and engineer because moving work between brains was expensive. Each boundary existed to manage a costly transfer: strategy into specs, specs into mockups, mockups into code. The roles crystallised around the seams.

AI just collapsed the cost of those transfers. And the roles are collapsing with them.

### What I'm seeing, concretely

Product managers prototype directly now — not wireframes, working software — and run their own research without waiting for three teams to align calendars.

Designers ship. Not "design systems that engineers implement" — actual pull requests, actual bug fixes.

Engineers write their own PRDs, talk to users, and make product calls with more context than the average PM had five years ago.

None of this is people heroically over-performing their role. It's the role boundaries becoming optional.

### From pipeline to loop

The old model was a pipeline: strategy → design → build → feedback, with a queue at every arrow. The queues were where products went to die.

The new model is a loop, and increasingly one person can run the entire loop in a day: notice a problem in the morning, prototype by lunch, test with users in the afternoon, ship by evening. The unit of execution is shrinking from "a team per quarter" to "a person per week."

That doesn't just change velocity. It changes hiring, team shape, and what "senior" even means. Seniority used to mean depth inside a boundary. It's starting to mean range across them.

### The uncomfortable part

I won't pretend to know the end state. Nobody knows what knowledge work looks like in ten years, and anyone who claims to is selling something.

But the direction is hard to miss: the people getting more valuable right now are not the ones defending their job description. They're hybrid operators — people who can think commercially, design clearly, and execute technically, in whatever order the problem demands.

Specialisation isn't dead. Deep expertise still compounds. What's dying is *role identity* — the idea that the boundary on your business card is a fact about you rather than an artifact of how expensive hand-offs used to be.

The hand-offs are gone. Plan accordingly.
